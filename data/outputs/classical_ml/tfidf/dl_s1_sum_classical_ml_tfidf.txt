Momentum
We add a term to the update rule which helps speed up learning and avoid getting stuck in local minima of the cost function
The term we add is a factor of the previous weight change. This has the effect of reducing the influence of the current iteration and “smoothing” training

Optimization Algorithms
SGD has issues:
Learning rates and schedules have to be set in advance, without knowing what may work best
Same learning rate applies to all weight updates each epoch
If our data is sparse we may want to update frequently occurring features less and rarely occurring features more
Alternatives
Adapt the learning rate to the weights
Image Source
Adagrad
Adapts the learning rate to each weight, at each time step
Lower LR (smaller updates) for weights of frequently occurring features and higher LR (larger updates) for weights of rare features
Well suited for sparse datasets (e.g. text)
Eliminates need to manually tune the LR
Weakness is that accumulated gradient in denominator eventually makes the step size too small to change weights
RMSprop
Alternative adaptive LR approach originally proposed by Geoffrey Hinton in his Coursera course
Attempt to resolve the radically diminishing learning rate in Adagrad
Adam
Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam)
Combination of the ideas behind RMSprop and momentum:
Uses both moving average of squared gradient (RMSprop) and moving average of the gradient (momentum)
Adam is generally regarded as the current “best” all-around optimizer
Other optimizers may work better for certain situations
E.g. Radam, Nadam, AdamW

Training Challenges
Challenges
Vanishing and Exploding Gradients
Getting stuck in local minima
Overfitting to training data
Long training cycles
When using activation functions like sigmoid or tanh:
These functions "squish" values into a range (0-1 for sigmoid)
Their derivatives are always less than 1 (maximum of 0.25 for sigmoid)
Vanishing Gradients
Wait, why do we care about the derivative of the activation function? When we multiply many small numbers together during backpropagation, the result gets exponentially smaller
In a best case scenario, each layer back reduces the gradient by roughly 75% (multiplied by 0.25)
By the time we reach the early layers, the gradient is practically zero
Vanishing Gradients
Vanishing Gradients
Simplified example to understand theory:
Start at the last layer (gradient = 1.0)
Move back to previous layer
Multiply by sigmoid derivative (let’s assume maximum derivative 0.25)
Multiply by weight (let’s assume weight ~1)
New gradient = ~1*0.25 = 0.25
Move back another layer
Multiply sigmoid derivative again (<0.25)
New gradient 0.25 * 0.25 = 0.0625
Move back another layer
Multiply sigmoid derivative again (<0.25)
New gradient 0.25 * 0.0625 = 0.015625
Repeat for n layers
Vanishing Gradients
Solutions:
Use ReLU activation function
Exploding gradients occur when large numbers multiply together
Most commonly happens with:
Large weight initializations (> 1.0)
Activation functions with derivatives ≥ 1 (like ReLU)
Effects compound in deep networks
Exploding Gradients
How to spot:
Loss fluctuates widely across training cycles
Model weights become very large or go to NaN
Solutions:
Reduce mini-batch size
Use gradient clipping – limit the size of gradients
Use batch norm

Regularization
Neural networks are prone to overfitting
We often use regularization techniques to reduce overfitting risk and improve convergence
Early Stopping
Image Source
The model will overfit at some point during training! We ideally want to stop training when the validation error is at a global minimum
Good: stop when val error begins to increase
Better: stop at global minimum of val error
Best: stop at point that gives best “price-performance” ratio (optimal ratio of training time to val error)
Early Stopping
Large Learning Rate
Larger LRs help avoid getting stuck in local minima, but create risk of not converging
Has been shown that large initial LRs reduce overfitting [Source]
LR Schedules
One strategy is to start with a higher learning rate and reduce it as training progresses and the weights approach the optimum
There are many methods we can use to decay the LR
With StepLR we decay the LR every step_size epochs by a factor gamma
One-cycle LR Policy
The 1cycle LR policy cycles the LR over the training, changing it after every batch
Small initial LR increases to a set max value and then decays back down to a small value
Momentum is cycled inversely to LR to balance regularization applied
Do LR schedulers play nice with Adam? Use of an adaptive optimizer may not be compatible with use of a learning rate scheduler
Benefits of adaptive optimizer are few/no hyperparameters to set
Benefit of LR scheduler (one-cycle policy) is potential for much quicker training (superconvergence)
In practice, try both – one-cycle policy with SGD, and Adam with no LR scheduler
Other forms of regularization can be used with adaptive optimizers:
Early stopping, weight decay, dropout, batch norm
L1 Regularization (Lasso) -
Adds a penalty equal to the absolute value of the magnitude of coefficients. Regularization via loss function
Batch Normalization
In backpropagation, weights of a layer are updated under the expectation that the prior layer outputs have a given distribution
In reality the distribution shifts due to variation across batches and updates to prior layer weights (internal covariate shift)
This slows learning by requiring lower learning rates and careful initialization of weights

BatchNorm standardizes the inputs of each layer, for each minibatch

Helps speed up and smooths training
